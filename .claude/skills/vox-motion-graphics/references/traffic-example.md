# Traffic explainer — complete worked brief and revision workflow

Use this reference when the user wants the traffic example demonstrated in the accompanying tutorial. It is a reusable production example, not a record of completed generations. Do not claim that a preview, approval, tool call, or export exists until it actually does.

## Scope and overrides

The general Skill defaults to a 60-second, 9:16 explainer in ten-second blocks. This example explicitly overrides those defaults:

- Topic: why traffic jams can form without an accident or other physical obstruction.
- Duration: exactly 30 seconds.
- Frame: 16:9.
- Structure: three consecutive ten-second blocks.
- Narration: English.
- Style: one approved editorial motion-graphics treatment across the entire film.
- Revision: give the initiating slowdown approximately one additional second within the same ten-second scene.

The example does not prescribe a particular voice, model, resolution, frame rate, or price. Use the user's choices and the verified capabilities of the connected tools. Record the agreed export settings before assembly. Do not silently change native resolution or advertise an upscale as native 4K.

## 2. Receive the creative assignment

The following brief is verbatim from the tutorial script:

> Create a thirty-second, sixteen-by-nine Vox-style explainer about why traffic jams happen without an accident. Use my motion graphics Skill and Higgsfield, with three ten-second blocks and English narration. Research the explanation, develop the script and visual direction, and produce one finished video. Show me the proposed script, scene plan, and style direction before production.

Prepare the proposal before production, as this brief explicitly requests. Include:

1. A concise explanation with sources and the central claim checked against them.
2. Narration designed to fit thirty seconds at a natural speaking pace.
3. Three timed scenes, showing how each spoken idea becomes visible action.
4. A proposed style direction and the elements that will remain consistent.
5. A production plan using the capabilities actually available.

A starting source to inspect is [MIT News: phantom traffic jams](https://news.mit.edu/2009/traffic-0609). Verify the explanation rather than treating this link as completed research. The visual mechanism should distinguish cars travelling forward from a slowdown that can propagate backward through dense traffic. Do not imply that every traffic jam has this cause.

The three-block story should establish the apparent mystery, show the initiating slowdown and following reactions, then resolve the mystery with the larger pattern. The scene plan should explain where the viewer looks, what changes first, how the effect spreads, and how the next shot continues that attention.

## 3. Approve the look and build the central scene

After the requested proposal has been reviewed and the applicable direction approved, create a shared visual reference covering the background, road, cars, textures, palette, and typography treatment. Reuse that approved reference across scenes.

Separate every production request into:

- **SHARED STYLE:** palette, textures, subject design, background treatment, and other approved constants.
- **SCENE / ACTION / CAMERA:** the scene-specific composition, motion sequence, timing, and camera framing.

A wider camera view changes the composition; it should not redesign the cars or replace the visual language. Keep readable captions and labels out of generated footage. Add final text during assembly. The reference can establish a type treatment without relying on a video model to render accurate lettering.

Build the central ten-second slowdown scene first for this tutorial workflow. Let the agent propose the framing and staging that make cause and reaction legible. The demonstrated approach begins close on the initiating car, shows its slowdown, then widens to reveal the following reactions. Do not describe that behavior as achieved until it is visible in the preview.

Review the first state, initiating action, chain reaction, and wider state at normal speed with the intended narration. Check that the first car remains recognizable as the composition opens. Save an unchanged **Scene 02 — Original** before revising it.

## 4. Finish the first cut and review transitions

Produce the opening and ending from the approved plan using the same reference. Assemble the three ten-second scenes against one continuous thirty-second narration track.

For each join:

1. Compare the last frame of the outgoing shot with the first frame of the incoming shot.
2. Identify the element the viewer is following and its position in both frames.
3. Check composition, subject identity, movement direction, and narration continuity.
4. Play through the join without a pause at normal speed.

Use a small temporary marker during analysis if it helps show the viewer's eye position. Remove review markers from the clean final film unless they serve the explanation itself. Save an unchanged **First Cut — Original** before applying the client revision.

## 5. Interpret the example client note

Display this as **EXAMPLE CLIENT NOTE**, not as a real client's testimonial or feedback record. The wording below is verbatim from the tutorial script:

> Can we spend a little longer on the first driver slowing down? I want people to notice that moment before we show the effect on everyone else.

The production request is also verbatim from the tutorial script:

> Revise the slowdown scene to emphasize the initiating action. Keep the first car’s slowdown clearly visible for about one extra second before expanding attention to the wider traffic pattern. Propose where to recover that time within the same scene. Preserve the narration, total scene duration, visual style, and neighboring scenes. Keep the original version for comparison.

This is a timing revision. It does not request reversing the reveal, replacing the narrator, rewriting the narration, redesigning the scene, or regenerating unaffected scenes.

## 6. Plan and implement the timing revision

Before making the alternative, inspect the real original scene and propose a measured time budget. Separate the initiating moment, chain reaction, and final hold. The chosen approach in this example is:

| Segment | Original | Revised |
|---|---|---|
| Initiating slowdown | Measured original duration | Approximately one second longer |
| Chain reaction | Measured original duration | Same duration and playback pace |
| Final hold | Measured original duration | Shorter by the time added to the initiating moment |
| Total scene | 10 seconds | 10 seconds |

Recover the time from the final hold rather than silently accelerating the chain reaction. Use measured source timings; do not invent values to fill this table. If the available final hold cannot safely supply the required time, explain the constraint and propose another change before claiming the example has been reproduced.

Once the timing approach is agreed, create **Scene 02 — Revised** while retaining the original. The additional emphasis must be visible in the actual clip. A timing table or title alone is not evidence that the motion was changed. Preserve the narration track and unaffected scenes. Record whether the change used editing, native animation, or a new generation; do not label an edit as a completed generation.

The corresponding approval prompt is:

> Use the shorter final hold to recover the extra second. Keep the chain reaction at its original pace, preserve the original version, and export the revised ten-second scene for comparison.

## 7. Compare original and revised versions

Prepare the following review views from the actual rendered files:

### ORIGINAL, then REVISED

Play each version from the same scene start with the same framing and narration. Use the labels **ORIGINAL** and **REVISED**. Check that the additional time on the first car is visible.

### Reaction pace

Display the measured timing bars. Then align the start of the chain reaction in both clips and play those sections side by side at their normal speed. This comparison checks the reaction pace; it has a different synchronization point from the whole-scene comparison.

### Full-scene duration

Start both full versions at scene time zero under the same narration. Use a common counter and show that both reach ten seconds while allocating time differently. Do not alter playback speed to force the durations to match.

Choose the version based on the actual result and the client note. The tutorial's intended choice is the revision because it makes the initiating action easier to notice while leaving time for the rest of the explanation. Do not announce that conclusion before inspecting the rendered alternative.

Replace only the central scene in the full assembly and play through both neighboring joins at normal speed.

## 8. Add final typography and sound

Use the approved type hierarchy: a larger main idea and smaller explanatory labels. For this example, a restrained label can identify the first slowdown; directional markers can distinguish forward vehicle motion from backward propagation of the slowdown. Keep wording consistent with the narration. Check reading time at normal playback speed and avoid covering the relevant cars.

Preserve a clean narration stem. Add music and purposeful sound effects as separate controllable elements. Duck the music beneath speech. A braking cue may emphasize the initiating action, but it must not mask the words explaining it. Check the complete sequence for intelligibility, clipping, abrupt changes, and excessive effects.

The sound-direction prompt is:

> Duck the music slightly under the voiceover. Add a braking sound at the first slowdown while keeping the words clear. Show the same passage first with narration only, then with the full mix.

Prepare a sound comparison using one short excerpt:

1. Play the excerpt with narration alone, labeled **VOICEOVER ONLY**.
2. Repeat the identical picture and time range with narration, music, and effects, labeled **FULL MIX**.

Use the same narration track, timing, and gain in both versions. Do not independently normalize the two versions or change the picture to make the comparison more dramatic. If demonstrated within a presenter-led tutorial, keep the presenter silent during the comparison.

If the only available source is a finished mix, separate the voice only when necessary and identify it as an extracted stem. Inspect it for missing words and residual music or effects. Do not describe extracted audio as an untouched original recording. For new production, keep the original clean voiceover from the outset.
